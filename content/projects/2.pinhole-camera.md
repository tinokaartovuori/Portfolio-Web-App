There are no electronics in it. Film advance, the shutter and the release
are all mechanical, and almost every part came off a printer. The only
parts I did not design are the bolts, the steel spring, the cable release and
the metal tripod thread.

The optics mount on a bayonet at the front. I made two pinholes for it, a
40 mm f/150 and a 52 mm f/192, and a Sony E-mount adapter lets it take a real
lens, so the same body shoots soft pinhole frames one day and a sharp 35 mm the
next. The pinholes were the hard part of the maths: the hole, the focal
length, the f-stop and the frame all depend on each other, and getting every
one of them to land in the right place inside the body took a good deal of
working out.

The grip comes off. Swapping it changes how the camera fires: one grip has a
plain button, another takes a cable release, and a new one can be printed for
whatever else is wanted. Underneath there is a metal tripod thread, so the
long pinhole exposures can be shot from a tripod.

The biggest challenge was none of that, though. It was the film: getting it
to move through the body smoothly and by exactly one frame at a time, with
printed parts alone. That is where most of the dozens of
versions and the dozens of smaller prototypes went, each printed to prove one
mechanism before it went into the body. Three full cameras came out of that;
the third is the MkIII in the pictures, and the photographs at the end came
out of it.
